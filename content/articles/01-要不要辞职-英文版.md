# Should I Quit My Job? Three Decisions to Make Before You Ask Anyone Else

You've been opening the job boards more often lately.

Not because you're looking for a job. Because you want to confirm there are still positions out there. You scroll for ten minutes, find a couple that look plausible, feel slightly better, and close the app. Two days later you do it again.

You may have already sent out a few applications. You know they aren't quite right. You sent them so you'd get a reply, so the whole thing would feel like it's moving. You may have started talking to one or two people you trust, and the way you put it was: "Just looking, I'm not set on leaving."

You've been weighing this for a while. Three months, maybe six, maybe longer.

What you want isn't really an answer. It's a reason good enough that you stop going back and forth. So you searched "should I quit my job" to see how other people decided.

This piece won't hand you an answer. It gives you three things: a way to locate yourself, three wrong turns to dismantle in advance, and one action you can take tonight. Every judgment about choosing comes from something that actually happened in the historical record, with the source named in each case.

---

## Part One: Find Out Which Kind of Stuck You Are

"Should I quit" isn't one question. It's six completely different situations wearing the same sentence. Which one you're in determines what your first move should be. Read the six below and start with the one that sounds most like you.

### One: You haven't been hurt yet. You've only heard rumors.

**Where you are**: A reorg is coming. The industry is whispering. A project might get cut. But nothing has actually landed on you — nobody has called you in, there's no document, no date. You can't sit still anymore. You're asking around, sending out resumes, wondering whether to jump first.

**Your first move**: Do your current work at its normal pace, and start a list only you can see. Write down every rumor you hear and where it came from. Look at that list three months from now. Most of those rumors will never have happened.

The real cost during a rumor phase isn't a missed opportunity. It's that you wreck your own rhythm. Working while interviewing means doing neither well. Then the rumor passes and your position has quietly loosened anyway.

**The closest case in the histories**: Right before Yue destroyed Wu, King Fuchai of Wu was at his most powerful, campaigning north against Qi. Wu Zixu kept warning him that Yue was the disease in the vital organ and Qi was a skin rash, that Yue had to be dealt with first. The king didn't listen. He listened to the sycophant Bo Pi instead.

What Wu Zixu did was correct. Before leaving on a mission, he told his son: *"I have remonstrated with the king many times and he does not use my advice. I now see the destruction of Wu. Your dying with Wu would be pointless."* Then he entrusted his son to the Bao family of Qi. (*Shiji*, vol. 66, "Biographies of Wu Zixu")

What he did was: state the facts plainly, secure his own people's exit, then go back and report. He didn't run, and he didn't pretend nothing was wrong. That's the shape of the right move during a rumor phase — keep records, know your facts, secure your people. Not hand in a resignation at midnight.

### Two: You're already being hurt, but the wound is still treatable.

**Where you are**: You've been sidelined, had credit taken, been criticized in front of others, been assigned work that was never yours. You can't name what's wrong, but something sits badly. Your boss didn't invite you to the last two meetings. A colleague started taking your reports to lunch.

**Your first move**: Stop looking for an explanation. Look at three things instead — money, people, information. Who has started spending around you, who has started meeting your people alone, who has stopped syncing information with you. If two of the three line up, you aren't imagining it.

**There's a numeric threshold here**: When you've felt "something's off with this person" three times in a row, stop looking for explanations. Three repetitions means a pattern, not a mood.

**The closest case in the histories**: How Han Xin ended.

After the battle of Gaixia, the empire was settled. Someone accused Han Xin, King of Chu, of plotting rebellion. Liu Bang used Chen Ping's stratagem — a fake imperial tour of inspection — and summoned Han Xin to meet him at Chen. Han Xin hesitated. Someone advised him: *"Cut off Zhongli Mei's head and present it to the emperor. He will be pleased and you will have no trouble."* Han Xin did it, carrying the head to Liu Bang, who had his guards seize him on the spot.

The words Han Xin said as he was being bound became a proverb: *"As they say — when the cunning rabbit dies, the hunting dog is cooked; when the high-flying birds are gone, the fine bow is put away; when the enemy state is destroyed, the strategist perishes. The empire is settled; I deserve to be cooked."* (*Zizhi Tongjian*, vol. 11, Han ji 3)

What happened next matters more. Liu Bang pardoned him and demoted him from King of Chu to Marquis of Huaiyin. From that moment Han Xin was safe. He no longer posed a threat. Liu Bang had no reason to kill him.

And what did Han Xin do? The *Tongjian* records: *"Xin knew the King of Han feared and disliked his abilities. He often claimed illness and did not attend court. He was constantly sullen, ashamed to be ranked alongside [Zhou Bo and Guan Ying]."*

He stayed home claiming illness, brooding, treating it as an insult to stand in the same rank as men like Zhou Bo and Guan Ying. Once he visited Fan Kuai, who knelt to greet him, called himself a subject, and said, "How gracious of the King to condescend to visit his servant!" Han Xin walked out and laughed: *"I have ended up keeping company with men like Fan Kuai!"*

Later Liu Bang asked him: how many troops do you think I can command? Han Xin said, no more than a hundred thousand. And you? Han Xin said: *"The more the better."*

Han Xin's real mistake wasn't the day he was bound. It was every year after the demotion. **His position had changed. His posture hadn't.** He had gone from "a man who must be guarded against" to "a man who could live" — but he still spoke, acted, and sulked as though he ought to be a king.

His end is in *Zizhi Tongjian*, vol. 12: *"Empress Lü had the guards bind Xin and behead him in the Bell Chamber. As Xin was being executed he said: 'I regret not using Kuai Che's plan — to be tricked by a woman, is this not Heaven's will?' His entire clan was exterminated."*

Sima Guang's judgment on this is the sharpest passage in the whole *Tongjian* on the question of whether to leave: *"To seize the moment and grab for profit is the ambition of a marketplace trader; to expect repayment for merit is the heart of a gentleman. Xin used a trader's ambition to benefit himself, yet expected a gentleman's heart from others — was that not difficult?"* (*Zizhi Tongjian*, vol. 12, Han ji 4)

He measured himself by the merchant's standard — go where the advantage is — while demanding that others meet the gentleman's standard: I did you a great service, you owe me. You can't run both.

This holds for you right now. When you assess whether to stay, you're using "how much have I given this company." When you assess the company, you're using "what can it give me." Two different rulers. You will never get a clear number that way.

### Three: A better opportunity is waving from a distance, but you can't step away from where you are.

**Where you are**: Higher pay, a bigger platform, a truer path — all sitting there in plain sight. But your project can't run without you, the team you built is still new, you promised someone something you haven't finished. You can't tell whether you're unwilling to leave or unable to.

**Your first move**: Don't compare who pays more. Compare who was already paving the road for you back when you had nothing to give them. The first is a transaction. The second is a position.

**And one more**: If the distant opportunity gives you no substantive response within a year — no date, no terms, no commitment — that isn't bad timing, that's a no. Time to move.

**The closest case in the histories**: Li Si.

In the thirty-fourth year of Qin Shi Huang's reign, Li Si's eldest son Li You was governor of Sanchuan and came home to Xianyang on leave. Li Si held a banquet. Every senior official came to offer congratulations. The carriages at his gate numbered in the thousands.

At that banquet, Li Si sighed:

*"Alas! I heard it from Xun Qing: 'Things must not flourish to the utmost.' I am a commoner from Shangcai, a black-haired common man from the alleys. The emperor did not know my poor abilities and raised me to this. Among all the emperor's subjects there is none ranked above me — one could say wealth and honor have reached their limit. **When things reach their limit they decline, and I do not know where to unhitch the carriage.**"* (*Shiji*, vol. 87, "Biography of Li Si")

*"I do not know where to unhitch the carriage."* He said it out loud. At the summit of his power, at the peak of the celebration, he named the pattern and admitted he didn't know how to stop.

**He saw it. Then he did nothing.**

Three years later Qin Shi Huang died at Shaqiu. Zhao Gao withheld the edict naming Fusu heir and came to persuade Li Si to install Hu Hai instead. Li Si's first reaction was correct: *"Where do these words that would destroy a state come from! This is not what a subject ought to discuss!"*

Zhao Gao asked the decisive question: *"Consider for yourself — in ability, who compares to Meng Tian? In merit, who compares to Meng Tian? In long-range judgment, who compares to Meng Tian? In being free of resentment from the world, who compares to Meng Tian?"* (*Shiji*, vol. 87)

All four questions are "how do you stack up against Meng Tian." Li Si wasn't weighing right against wrong. He was weighing whether Fusu's accession would cost him his post. He agreed.

The rest: In the seventh month of the second year of Qin Er Shi, *"Li Si was subjected to the five punishments and sentenced to be cut in half at the waist in the Xianyang marketplace. Coming out of prison, bound together with his second son, he turned and said to him: '**I want to go with you again, leading the yellow dog, out the east gate of Shangcai to chase the cunning rabbit — how could that ever be!**' Father and son wept together, and his clan to the third degree was exterminated."* (*Shiji*, vol. 87)

The thing to notice isn't how badly it ended. It's the gap in time. When he said *"I do not know where to unhitch the carriage,"* he held the chancellorship, the allegiance of every official, and the full range of options. When he said *"leading the yellow dog out the east gate of Shangcai,"* he was in a prison cart.

**Three years separated those two sentences.**

Li Si's problem wasn't that he failed to see it. It's that while he could still leave, he treated it as a thought. By the time he had to leave, there was no such thing anymore.

If you're currently comparing two options, remember this: the reasons that keep you from leaving — the project needs you, you built the team, you promised someone — look most important precisely while you can still leave with your dignity. By the time you're forced out, not one of them will still be standing.

### Four: You've put in too much to walk away.

**Where you are**: Money, time, health, your relationships at home — all of it is riding on this one thing. You can't see a road forward and you can't bear to write off the cost of turning back. You want to cry, but what would crying change?

**Your first move**: When you can no longer say clearly what will be better about this three months from now, do three things immediately. Write out what you've already put in — money, time, favors, each with a number. Set a definite stop-loss date. Put the option of selling or merging this thing onto the table.

**And a harder self-check**: Take a sheet of paper tonight and write down everything you've invested. Then ask yourself one question — **if I heard about this today for the first time, knowing what I know now, would I still invest?** If the answer is no, you are standing in sunk cost. Stop adding to it.

Delay is the most expensive additional investment there is, and it pays no return.

**The closest case in the histories**: Fan Li and Wen Zhong — same ruler, same war, same achievement, two endings.

After Yue destroyed Wu, Fan Li was made Supreme General. What he did next: *"Fan Li considered that beneath so great a name it is hard to remain long, and that Goujian was a man who could share adversity but not prosperity."* So he wrote a letter of resignation to Goujian: *"I have heard that when the ruler is anxious the minister labors, and when the ruler is shamed the minister dies. When Your Majesty was shamed at Kuaiji, the reason I did not die was for this matter. Now that the humiliation is wiped away, I ask to receive the punishment due at Kuaiji."*

Goujian's reply was a threat: *"I will divide the state with you and you shall hold it. If not, I will have you executed."*

Fan Li's answer was six characters, and they may be the six most useful characters in this entire piece: *"**Your Majesty issues orders; your servant follows his own intent.**"*

The *Shiji* continues: *"He then packed his light treasures and pearls, and with his own private followers boarded a boat and floated out to sea, never to return."* (*Shiji*, vol. 41, "Hereditary House of Goujian, King of Yue")

He went to Qi, changed his name to Chi Yi Zi Pi, farmed by the sea, and built a fortune father-and-son. Within no great time he had amassed several hundred thousand. The people of Qi heard of his worth and made him chancellor. He served a short while, then sighed again: *"At home I earn a thousand pieces of gold; in office I reach the rank of minister and chancellor. This is the summit for a common man. To hold a lofty name too long is inauspicious."* He returned the seal of office, scattered his wealth among friends and neighbors, took his treasures, and left quietly for Tao, calling himself Lord Zhu of Tao. (*Shiji*, vol. 41)

**The same man, leaving three times.** Every departure at the peak.

Wen Zhong didn't leave. Fan Li had sent word to him. Wen Zhong stayed. On his end, the clearest line in the *Shiji* is in volume 83, where Zou Yang writes from prison: *"Qin used Shang Yang's laws, weakened Han and Wei in the east, made its armies the strongest in the world — and in the end tore him apart with chariots. **Yue used the Grand Officer Wen Zhong's stratagems, seized mighty Wu, and became hegemon over the central states — and in the end put him to death.**"* (*Shiji*, vol. 83, "Biographies of Lu Zhonglian and Zou Yang")

Later, when Cai Ze was arguing with the Qin chancellor Fan Ju, he distilled the whole thing into three sentences — the most practical test in this piece:

*"A sage ruler and a worthy minister are the great fortune of the world. A discerning ruler and an upright minister are the fortune of a state. A compassionate father and a filial son, a faithful husband and a chaste wife, are the fortune of a household. Yet Bi Gan was loyal and could not preserve Yin; Wu Zixu was wise and could not preserve Wu; Shen Sheng was filial and Jin fell into chaos. **In each case there were loyal ministers and filial sons, and yet the state was destroyed and thrown into disorder — why? Because there was no enlightened ruler or wise father to listen to them.**"* (*Shiji*, vol. 79, "Biographies of Fan Ju and Cai Ze")

Cai Ze then ranked the three possible outcomes: *"**To preserve both body and name is the highest. To have a name worth emulating while the body dies is next. To have the name disgraced while the body survives is the lowest.**"* (ibid.)

Body and name both intact is the top outcome. A name worth emulating but a dead body is second. A ruined name and a living body is last.

What you're deciding isn't "am I right to do this." It's **which of those three your situation is heading for.** Whether the person above you can hear unwelcome news is not something you need to guess. You already have half a year of samples.

### Five: There's a big opportunity in front of you. Taking it means a different world.

**Where you are**: The opportunity is large. You also know that if this jump misses, the fall is far worse than where you stand now. You've been weighing it for a long time.

**Your first move**: Before taking something bigger than your current position, answer one question on a single sheet of paper — when the worst arrives, **which of these will move: my income, my housing, the three people closest to me?**

**The numeric threshold**: If two of the three hold, jump. If all three move, build the fallback first, then jump.

**The exception**: If the opportunity is strongly time-bound — there will be no second one a year from now — and the maximum cost of failure is some time and an amount of money you can absorb, then stop calculating the fallback and jump. People who calculate too carefully spend their whole lives on the shore.

**The closest case in the histories**: Fan Ju handing the Qin chancellorship to Cai Ze.

Cai Ze was an itinerant persuader who had been rejected everywhere. Driven out of Zhao. Robbed of his cooking pots on the road through Han and Wei. He heard that Fan Ju, chancellor of Qin, felt uneasy because the two men he had recommended, Zheng Anping and Wang Ji, had both committed grave crimes. So he went west to Qin. He first let word spread: *"Cai Ze of Yan is the most formidable and eloquent strategist in the world. If he once meets the King of Qin, the king will certainly corner you and take your position."* Fan Ju summoned him and asked whether he had said it. Cai Ze said yes.

His opening line to the chancellor was one sentence: *"**By the order of the four seasons, whoever succeeds must depart.**"* (*Shiji*, vol. 79)

He then named three men: Shang Yang of Qin, Wu Qi of Chu, the Grand Officer Wen Zhong of Yue. Fan Ju initially defended all three, calling them "the utmost of righteousness, the integrity of loyalty," men who "regard death as a return home." Cai Ze asked him: would you yourself accept their fate? Fan Ju said no. Cai Ze said: then what are you waiting for.

The result: *"Fan Ju said: 'Excellent.' He then invited him in, seated him, and made him an honored guest. Some days later he went to court and said to King Zhao of Qin: 'A guest newly arrived from the east named Cai Ze — he is a man of great eloquence, versed in the affairs of the three kings and the achievements of the five hegemon, and in the changes of the age. He is fit to be entrusted with the government of Qin. I have seen many men and none compares. I dare not withhold this from Your Majesty.'"* Fan Ju then pleaded illness and resigned the chancellorship, recommending Cai Ze to succeed him. (*Shiji*, vol. 79, "Biographies of Fan Ju and Cai Ze")

**Fan Ju did something very few people do: while he still held power, he handed it over of his own accord.** And he did it cleanly — not pushed out, but standing in court saying it himself.

The value of this case is that it proves stepping down at the top is a real, workable move, not a moral fable invented afterward.

### Six: You've decided. But you're hung about with things.

**Where you are**: You've made the call. But there's work you haven't handed over, people who work for you, a promise already given. You want to leave cleanly, and you've discovered that the more you try to leave beautifully, the less you can leave at all.

**Your first move**: Separate two things — **is it the people you can't let go of, or the status?**

**The closest case in the histories**: Zhang Liang.

After Liu Bang settled the empire, he enfeoffed Zhang Liang as Marquis of Liu. What Zhang Liang did takes a few lines in the *Shiji*: *"The Marquis of Liu then declared: 'My family served Han for generations; when Han was destroyed I did not begrudge ten thousand pieces of gold, and I avenged Han against mighty Qin, shaking the world. Now with a three-inch tongue I have been teacher to an emperor, enfeoffed as a marquis with ten thousand households — **this is the summit for a common man, and for me it is enough. I wish to cast off the affairs of the world and wander with Master Red Pine.**' He then studied grain avoidance and the arts of guiding and stretching to lighten the body."* (*Shiji*, vol. 55, "Hereditary House of the Marquis of Liu")

*"The summit for a common man."* This is as far as it goes. This is enough.

The *Tongjian* records it more precisely and appends Sima Guang's judgment: *"Zhang Liang had always been sickly. After following the emperor into the pass, he practiced guiding exercises, ate no grain, and **shut his door and did not go out**."* (*Zizhi Tongjian*, vol. 11, Han ji 3)

Sima Guang's judgment: *"Between merit and fame is the hardest place for a minister to stand. Those the emperor named were only three men. **The Marquis of Huaiyin [Han Xin] was executed; Xiao He was imprisoned — was it not because they stood at the fullest point and did not stop?** Zhang Liang therefore took refuge in the immortals, cast off the human world, treated merit and fame as external things, and set glory and profit aside — of 'the wise man who preserves himself,' Zhang Liang had it."* (ibid.)

Sima Guang is comparing three men under the same ruler. Han Xin executed. Xiao He imprisoned. Zhang Liang died of old age.

**The difference was not the size of their merit. It was whether the stopping was done.**

None of Zhang Liang's obligations were abandoned. He didn't vanish. He "shut his door and did not go out" — the door closed, the man still there, still advising when needed. When Liu Bang wanted to replace the crown prince and Empress Lü begged him for a solution, Zhang Liang still acted. He brought in the Four Whiteheads of Mount Shang. **He simply stopped wanting a position.**

The phrase to take from this is "shut his door and did not go out." Zhang Liang's method was to move slowly and with dignity from the center to the edge while he still carried weight — not to announce that he was done tomorrow.

### And one more: if you're already in a position you look down on

The six above are about whether to leave. There's another kind of person whose problem isn't whether to leave but **why you're still there at all.**

The rule for this situation reads: *"The work you're doing now makes you feel ashamed. Maybe you're taking the blame for someone. Maybe you're writing copy you know is dishonest. Maybe you sit through meetings you know are pointless. You can't quite say how you got here."*

The historical case is Mu Sheng.

Liu Bang's younger brother Liu Jiao was enfeoffed as King Yuan of Chu, and he held three scholars in high regard: Mu Sheng, Shen Gong, and Bai Sheng. Mu Sheng didn't drink wine, so every time King Yuan held a banquet he had a special sweet wine set out for Mu Sheng alone. King Yuan died and his son Liu Wu succeeded. At first the practice continued. Then it was forgotten.

The *Han Shu* records Mu Sheng's reaction: *"Mu Sheng withdrew and said: 'It is time to go. **The sweet wine is no longer set out; the king's intention has slackened. If I do not leave, the men of Chu will put the cangue on me in the marketplace.**' He claimed illness and stayed in bed."*

Shen Gong and Bai Sheng urged him: how can you leave over a small courtesy? Have you forgotten the former king's kindness? Mu Sheng said: *"The Yi says, 'To know the incipient is to be divine. The incipient is the subtle movement of things, the first appearance of good or ill fortune. The gentleman acts when he sees the incipient; he does not wait for the day to end.' The former king honored the three of us because the Way was present. Now it is neglected, which means the Way is forgotten. **With one who forgets the Way, how can one remain long?** Is it for a trifling courtesy?"*

Then *"he pleaded illness and departed. Shen Gong and Bai Sheng stayed."*

What followed: Liu Wu grew increasingly violent and dissolute, was stripped of Donghai and Xue commandaries for a private sexual offense, and conspired with the King of Wu to rebel. Shen Gong and Bai Sheng remonstrated. Liu Wu didn't listen. He *"had them put in bonds, dressed them in the red garments of convicts, and made them hull grain with pestle and mortar in the marketplace."* (*Han Shu*, vol. 36, "Biography of the Prince of Chu Yuan")

Mu Sheng's evidence wasn't the cup of wine. It was this: **the former king set out the wine because the Way was present; now that it is forgotten, the Way is gone.** He was reading what had changed behind the behavior, not the behavior.

*"With one who forgets the Way, how can one remain long?"* You can take that sentence and use it directly. Whether a place is still worth staying in isn't measured by whether they're polite to you. It's measured by whether they still care about the thing that brought you together.

## Part Two: The Three Most Common Misjudgments

Part One locates you. But locating yourself isn't enough. **Most people get "should I quit" wrong not because they lack information, but because they're measuring with the wrong ruler.**

Each of the three below has a historical case set against it.

### Misjudgment One: Mistaking "I can't accept this" for "there's still a chance"

The longer you spend on something, the more valuable it feels. That's basic wiring. The investment changes your evaluation of the thing — not because you've seen new evidence, but because you don't want to admit the earlier investment was wasted.

**The test**: Write down three reasons you think it still has a chance. Then ask of each one — **is this a fact that appeared this week, or something I already knew three months ago?** If all three are old, you aren't holding new evidence. You're holding resentment.

**The case**: Faced with the reality that he lost on all four comparisons against Meng Tian, Li Si chose to install Hu Hai. What he did was not judgment, it was damage control — what he feared was his position under Fusu, not what happened to Qin. He swapped "can this thing still be done" for "can I keep my post." That's resentment replacing judgment. (*Shiji*, vol. 87)

### Misjudgment Two: Treating sunk cost as though it hasn't sunk

Once money, time, and relationships are invested, they are no longer yours. What you control is only the part remaining from today forward.

**The test**: The same question as before — **if I heard about this today for the first time, knowing what I know now, would I still invest?** That single question dissolves about ninety percent of the agonizing, because it doesn't ask about the past. It only asks about the future.

**The case**: Wen Zhong stayed not because he had a judgment about Yue's future, but because he was the architect of Wu's destruction and the merit was sitting right there in plain view. He could not admit the thing was over. (*Shiji*, vol. 83)

### Misjudgment Three: Mistaking "putting up with it for now" for "strategic patience"

The difference between these two is exactly one thing: **whether you've written a number for it.**

Strategic patience has a trigger — how many consecutive days without progress, how many months of cash remaining, which key metric falling below what level. When the number hits, you withdraw. No further meeting required.

Putting up with it for now has no condition, only a mood. It hurts each time you remember it. You endure, it hurts again, you endure again — until one day it erupts, and the decision you make then is not the one you wanted.

**The test**: **What is the exit condition for the thing you're currently enduring?** If you can't write down a number, you aren't being strategically patient. You're just enduring.

**The case**: Wu Zixu stayed at the side of King Fuchai of Wu, remonstrating again and again, until he was sent the sword Shulou to kill himself. His parting instruction to his retainers: *"**Plant catalpa on my grave so it can be made into implements; and gouge out my eyes and hang them above the east gate of Wu, so that I may watch the Yue armies enter and destroy Wu.**"* (*Shiji*, vol. 66)

He endured for years. His endurance had no condition, so it had no endpoint.

**And the counter-case — when staying is right**: Shu Guang and Shu Shou.

Under Emperor Xuan of Han, Shu Guang was Grand Tutor to the crown prince and his nephew Shu Shou was Junior Tutor. Uncle and nephew teaching the crown prince together — "the court considered it an honor." After five years the crown prince was twelve and had mastered the *Analects* and the *Classic of Filial Piety*.

Then Shu Guang said to Shu Shou: *"I have heard that 'to know sufficiency is to avoid disgrace; to know when to stop is to avoid danger,' and that 'when the work is accomplished, to withdraw the person is the Way of Heaven.' **Now we have reached the rank of two thousand bushels; our official names are made and our reputations established. If we do not leave under these conditions, I fear there will be regret later.** Would it not be better for uncle and nephew to leave the pass together, return to our home village, and end our lives there?"* (*Han Shu*, vol. 71, "Biographies of Juan, Shu, Yu, Xue, Ping, and Peng")

Shu Shou bowed his head and said: "I follow your judgment." That same day both pleaded illness. After three months they took leave, Shu Guang claimed grave illness, and submitted a memorial begging to be allowed to retire with his bones. The emperor, considering them advanced in years, approved both, adding a gift of twenty catties of gold, with the crown prince adding fifty more. Ministers, nobles, old friends, and townsmen held a farewell feast; the carriages seeing them off outside the East Gate numbered several hundred. Onlookers along the road said, *"How worthy, the two grandees!"* Some sighed and wept for them. (*Han Shu*, vol. 71)

Once home, Shu Guang had food and wine set out daily, inviting clan members, old friends, and guests to share it, repeatedly asking how much gold remained and urging that it be sold to pay for the spread. After more than a year, his sons and grandsons had someone suggest he buy land and property. Shu Guang's answer: *"Do you think I am old and foolish and do not think of my descendants? I have old fields and a house; let my descendants work hard in them, which is enough for food and clothing, on the same level as ordinary people. To add more now as surplus would only teach my descendants idleness. **If they are worthy and have much wealth, it diminishes their resolve; if they are foolish and have much wealth, it increases their faults.**"* (*Han Shu*, vol. 71)

Uncle and nephew *"both ended their lives in old age."*

**Mu Sheng and Shu Guang are two faces of the same kind of person.** Mu Sheng left when he saw the Way was gone. Shu Guang left when he saw the work was done. Both left before they were forced to.

## Part Three: One Thing You Can Do Tonight

Not "think it over carefully." One thing you can do tonight.

### Tonight: Write the "worst outcome" sheet

Take a sheet of paper. Draw three columns: **income, housing, people.**

In each column write specific names and numbers, not concepts.

- **Income**: Not "it will decrease," but "from X down to Y, sustainable for N months."
- **Housing**: The actual address and the monthly payment, and how long this place holds if income stops.
- **People**: Write the names of three specific people — the ones you could call at your worst. Not the number of contacts in your phone.

Then draw a line at the bottom and write one sentence below it: **which of these three will move.**

**The rule:**

- **None of the three moves**: What you're agonizing over isn't risk, it's mood. Go do the thing.
- **Exactly one moves**: Take that one column and write your concrete response plan — not "figure something out," but "what I do in week one, what I do in month one." If you can write the plan, go. If you can't, build the plan first.
- **Two or more move**: Don't treat quitting as a single action. Break it into a sequence of prior actions. Restore the income line first, then talk about leaving.

**Why these three**: The original rule reads — *"Before taking an opportunity beyond your current position, answer one question on a single sheet of paper: when the worst arrives, which of my income, my housing, and the three people closest to me will move. If two of the three hold, jump. If all three move, build the fallback first, then jump."*

### Tomorrow: Write a number for what you're enduring

If you decide to stay for now, you must attach a **deadline and a condition** to that decision. The original rule: *"The decision not to leave must come with a definite deadline and condition."*

Write it in this format, as specifically as you can:

> I am staying in my current position until ______ (date).
> Before then, if ______ (one specific thing) happens, I will reassess.
> If by that date ______ (another specific thing) has not happened, I begin looking in earnest.

"I've decided to stay" without that line means you decided nothing. Three months from now you'll be standing in the same place asking the same question.

### If you've already decided to leave: three rules for leaving

1. **Tell your direct manager in person at least a month ahead**, then start the process. Give one reason. Don't over-explain. Don't complain about specific people or incidents. Dignity isn't a performance for others — it determines whether anyone will still take your call three years from now.
2. **Finish the handover document, return the keys and access, and tell the people who still contact you.** Whether you left cleanly is measured by whether you're still inside this thing three months later.
3. **Deal with three things**: the title you still hold, the group chats you're still in, the advisory calls you still take. Keeping a nominal role means keeping a rope that can pull you back at any time.

## Finally: If You Still Don't Know What to Do

Everything above is a set of judgment tools. It can break the question apart. But there's a category where tools don't help — **you don't lack judgment. You can't produce one.**

Two specific situations:

**One: You don't have enough information, and you can't get it yourself.**
Say you're locked in a struggle with someone far above your level. You have right on your side, but the referee, the rules, and the resources are all his. The question isn't "should I leave," it's "can I win." Judging that requires someone to help you inventory three things: who can serve as referee, what the referee's relationship to the other party is, and whether you can preserve your income, your housing, and your people if you lose.

**Two: You're choosing between two things that are both right, and you've already been through it many times.**
You keep going back over it because you've calculated the pros and cons of both, and they simply aren't commensurable. Another hundred passes won't produce an answer. What you need is someone to restate your situation from outside — using an angle you haven't tried.

**When not to contact me:**

- You just want someone to confirm your decision is right. I can't supply that, and neither can anyone else. Anything you get is fake.
- You aren't at a decision point yet and just want to talk it through. Talk to a friend. Friends are cheaper.
- You want me to tell you quit or don't quit. I don't do that. Anyone who makes that call for you is being irresponsible.

**When it makes sense to contact me:**
You've finished the "worst outcome" sheet and filled in the number for what you're enduring, and you're still stuck — not because you don't know what to do, but because every option in front of you looks roughly the same, or all of them look bad.

Send me the sheet. I won't give you an answer. I'll do three things:

**First**, match your situation against the 384 situations and rules. The classification isn't mysticism — it's derived from the positional structure of the sixty-four hexagrams, where each position corresponds to a specific place in an organizational structure. Things like "you're the number two, but the thing collapses within three months without you," or "you've been pushed into the middle where neither side will accept you." It's unlikely your problem is entirely new.

**Second**, find a structurally equivalent case from twenty-eight official histories. Not "Zeng Guofan once said." A real person, in a specific volume of a specific history, facing a position structurally identical to yours — what he did and what happened after.

**Third**, help you fill in the column still missing from your two sheets.

**How to start**: Put the contents of your "worst outcome" sheet and your endurance number into one message and send it. Three hundred words is enough. Skip the backstory. Just write: what your position is, where you're stuck, what you've already tried, and what cards you're holding.
